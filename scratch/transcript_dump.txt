106: ## 3.3.1 Problem description and assumptions
107: 
108: Designing Brazil's national grain storage network requires planners and policymakers to determine long-term investment decisions for multiple facilities and operational flows for multiple products across a multi-period supply chain. Because the country relies primarily on road transport, moving increasing grain volumes creates logistical difficulties, especially since grain production regularly exceeds national static storage capacity. Reception and shipping capacities within grain warehouses are equally constrained, slowing throughput even when static storage space exists. During peak harvest periods, this deficit causes operational bottlenecks because grain must be processed and transported quickly to prevent spoilage and farm congestion.
109: 
110: Planners require an optimization model to evaluate the infrastructure and support logistics decisions. At the network level, this model must determine which candidate warehouses to open, select facility types such as silos or conventional warehouses, and establish their capacities. It must also identify existing facilities for expansion or conversion to bulk-handling operations and allocate grain products from origins through intermediate warehouses to domestic or export destinations. The complexity of these decisions, combined with uncertainty in harvest supply and demand, makes manual planning computationally intractable and economically suboptimal.
111: 
112: To formulate this optimization problem, the physical supply chain is abstracted as a directed tri-partite graph. The network comprises a set of origin nodes representing supply locations (farms or municipalities), a set of warehouse nodes representing existing and candidate storage facilities, and a set of customer nodes representing domestic and export demand points. Directed arcs connect origins to warehouses, warehouses to customers, and warehouses to other warehouses for interhub operations. Active arcs are generated exclusively for valid combinations satisfying commodity and infrastructure compatibility requirements.
113: 
114: The mathematical formulation relies on the following core assumptions:
115: 
116: - The supply volume available at each origin per product and per period is known. May be deterministic or stochastic.
117: - The domestic demand at each customer per product and per period is known. May be deterministic or stochastic.
118: - The export market is an uncapacitated sink with an infinite absorption capacity.
119: - All supply at each origin must be fully allocated across the network in each period.
120: - All domestic demand for each customer across the network must be fully met in each period.
121: - The static storage capacity and daily reception and shipping capacities of existing warehouses are known and deterministic.
122: - Transportation freight costs, road distances, and storage tariffs are known and deterministic.
123: - Fixed and variable capital costs for opening candidate warehouses, expanding existing warehouses, and converting facilities to bulk-handling are known and deterministic.
124: - Expansion and bulkification are mutually exclusive investment options.
125: - The geographical locations of existing and candidate warehouses sites are known and fixed.
126: - The planning horizon, expressed as the number of time periods and active operational days per period, is known.
127: 
128: Two MILP models are available for the planner: a deterministic and a stochastic extension. The deterministic model is a multi-facility, multi-product, multi-period hub location and allocation model. Warehouses act as hubs where interhub operations receive an interhub factor (which can discount or penalize the routing cost) to move grain stocks. Strategic decisions are binary and determine facility location, capacity expansion, and bulkification. Operational decisions optimize continuous flow allocations.
129: 
130: The stochastic extension retains these assumptions but treats supply volumes and domestic demand as scenario-dependent parameters across a finite set of discrete scenarios with known probabilities. First-stage facility investment decisions are scenario-independent and occur before uncertainty is realized. Second-stage recourse decisions determine operational flows and inventory independently for each scenario realization.

3: ## 3.1. Design Science Research
4: 
5: This study applies Design Science Research (DSR) to guide the development and evaluation of the SiloDSS decision support system. DSR is an applied research paradigm that focuses on creating and evaluating information technology artifacts to solve specific organizational problems (Hevner, 2007; Lacerda et al., 2013; Peffers et al., 2007). By adopting DSR, this research complements descriptive studies of agricultural supply chain logistics by actively constructing a practical solution for the strategic and tactical planning of Brazil's national grain storage network.
6: 
7: Adopting DSR, which categorizes artifacts into constructs, models, methods, and instantiations (Lacerda et al., 2013; Peffers et al., 2007), the SiloDSS system is defined as an instantiation. An instantiation is the concretization of an artifact in its target environment, functioning to operationalize theoretical constructs, models, and methods to test their viability and efficacy in practice (Lacerda et al., 2013). SiloDSS achieves this by integrating data acquisition, statistical and machine learning forecasting, and abstract mathematical programming models into an interactive web application. This instantiation allows institutional planners and policymakers to interact directly with advanced optimization and forecasting techniques without requiring prior modeling expertise.
8: 
9: Following a problem-centered initiation, the six-step Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) for information systems is applied to structure the research execution (Peffers et al., 2007). Fig. 1 illustrates the progression of these sequential steps as applied to the SiloDSS system. The steps are detailed as follows:
10: 
11: _Step 1: Problem identification and motivation._ Official statements by public sector managers and institutional data from the Brazilian National Supply Company (Conab) identify a persistent gap in the strategic planning of grain storage infrastructure. Brazil's grain production frequently exceeds national static storage capacity, creating logistical bottlenecks during peak harvest periods. Decisions regarding which warehouses to open, expand, or upgrade for bulk-grain handling involve high combinatorial complexity across hundreds of candidate facilities, multiple grain products, and a multi-year planning horizon — a scale that renders manual planning computationally intractable.
12: 
13: _Step 2: Definition of the objectives for a solution._ The primary objective is to develop an open-source DSS that optimizes multi-period grain storage network decisions — including facility location, capacity expansion, and bulkification — to minimize total logistical and infrastructure costs. The solution must support both deterministic and stochastic optimization to hedge strategic investment decisions against uncertainty in supply and demand. To facilitate daily operational use by agribusiness planners, the solution requires a free, accessible interface that abstracts the complexity of the underlying mathematical models, while integrating forecasting capabilities to assist in the generation of future planning scenarios.
14: 
15: _Step 3: Design and development._ The creation of the SiloDSS instantiation integrates a Python backend, utilizing Pyomo for MILP mathematical models, with a web-based GUI. Statistical forecasting (SARIMA, Prophet) and machine learning models (XGBoost, LSTM) are incorporated to enable multi-period supply and demand prediction directly within the system workflow. Furthermore, the structural and functional design incorporates established principles for the effective delivery of agricultural DSSs, including usability, workflow relevance, and system simplicity (Rose et al., 2016).
16: 
17: _Step 4: Demonstration._ The DSS is applied to a real macro-logistics case study using estimated supply, candidate warehouse, and demand data to assess the feasibility of the instantiated mathematical models in a real-world strategic planning scenario.
18: 
19: _Step 5: Evaluation._ The artifact is evaluated through two phases. First, computational performance and model limits are assessed via stress testing by incrementally scaling the number of supply, warehouse, and demand nodes. Second, its logistical and financial performance are evaluated through a case study, assessing the quality of the optimization solutions produced.
20: 
21: _Step 6: Communication._ The structuring and publication of this academic paper disseminates the design knowledge and the artifact to researchers and practitioners. Additionally, the complete codebase is made publicly available in an open-source repository to encourage further iterations and distribution.

25: ## 3.2. SiloDSS architecture and implementation
26: 
27: SiloDSS operates on a monorepo architecture that unifies the web application interface, a local Open Source Routing Machine<sup>[\[1\]](#footnote-1)</sup> (OSRM) service, and background mathematical optimization tasks. The system architecture and operational workflow are illustrated in Fig. 2, while Table 1 details the processes. Development occurred in a public GitHub repository<sup>[\[2\]](#footnote-2)</sup> using the artificial intelligence (AI) tools Gemini<sup>[\[3\]](#footnote-3)</sup> and Antigravity<sup>[\[4\]](#footnote-4)</sup> to improve the implementation. The codebase is structured around a GUI that serves as an abstraction layer, coordinating data transfer between the frontend and the backend logic modules. The frontend is built using the Plotly Dash framework<sup>[\[5\]](#footnote-5)</sup> and served via Gunicorn (WSGI) for production stability. The system incorporates an internationalization structure to power a multilingual dashboard, currently loading English or Brazilian Portuguese translation files based on user preference.

53: The development relies on Docker<sup>[\[6\]](#footnote-6)</sup> for containerization to guarantee environmental standardization and reproducibility across different operating systems. The use of Docker simplifies the deployment process, facilitating the initialization of the system and its dependencies, including the routing engine and mathematical solver, without the need for complex local configurations.
54: 
55: Designed for diverse user groups, the system incorporates an internationalization structure to power a multilingual dashboard. This structure currently loads English or Brazilian Portuguese translation files based on user preference and can be easily adapted to include additional languages.
56: 
57: To prioritize accessibility, and since all data is public, the system lowers entry barriers by functioning as a web application requiring no login or authentication. Consequently, user data persists only for the duration of the active session, with no configurations or results saved once the application is closed. The execution of the workflow relies on user-provided inputs managed across ten sequentially organized tabs. The backend data processing modules, primarily utilizing the Pandas and OpenPyXL libraries, ingest and structure this information to feed the subsequent forecasting, spatial, and optimization processes.
58: 
59: The first two tabs establish the planning horizon and collect historical monthly supply and demand data per product and municipality. The planning horizon — defined by a start and end year — is locked once data is loaded to maintain temporal consistency across all subsequent tabs. Data can be imported via Excel or CSV files or entered manually through inline forms supporting constant value and linear growth patterns. Fig. 3 illustrates the Demand tab interface.

61: **Fig. 3.** SiloDSS demand tab interface. The left panel provides controls for uploading files, adding rows manually, and exporting data. The right panel displays a monthly demand evolution chart filtered by product and city, an editable data table with all registered demand entries, and global summary KPIs for total demand volume and number of distinct products.
62: 
63: Following data collection, the forecasting step is mandatory prior to optimization. Users select one of four algorithms and configure two shared parameters: the validation split size (number of final months of historical data reserved for accuracy testing) and the future prediction horizon (number of months to forecast beyond the historical series). The system computes accuracy metrics (WMAPE, RMSE, MAE) alongside a qualitative performance rating and residual diagnostic plots to assist model assessment. The predicted values extend the supply and demand datasets and are used to populate the optimization model inputs. Each algorithm operates with fixed internal parameters: SARIMA fits an ARIMA(1,1,1) model, automatically upgrading to a seasonal SARIMAX(1,1,1)×(1,0,1,12) specification when the training series contains at least 24 months of data; Prophet applies yearly seasonality in additive mode with weekly and daily seasonality disabled; XGBoost uses an autoregressive feature structure with lags of 1, 2, and 3 months — extended to include a 12-month seasonal lag when at least 15 months of data are available — and is fitted with 50 trees, maximum depth 3, and a learning rate of 0.1; LSTM employs a single-layer recurrent network with 16 hidden units, trained for 100 epochs with the Adam optimizer, using a lookback sequence length set dynamically between 3 and 12 months based on training series length. Fig. 4 shows the forecasting interface.

67: The warehouse registration tab allows users to define both existing facilities and candidate locations. For each warehouse, users specify the structure type (e.g., Silo, Graneleiro, Convencional), static storage capacity, and daily reception and shipping limits. Two facility investment alternatives are configurable: expansion, which adds static storage capacity, and bulkification, which refers to the technological conversion of a warehouse to bulk-grain handling operations — a process that increases daily reception and shipping throughput rates without increasing static capacity. Opening costs for candidate facilities — those that do not yet exist but may be opened — are also specified at this stage. These investment parameters, combined with the freight and tariff costs defined in the Costs tab, jointly determine the investment and operational cost terms of the objective function. 71: With all facility and cost inputs established, users proceed to the Distance Matrix tab, where a single action triggers the OSRM engine to compute road distances for all three network corridors simultaneously: Supply→Warehouses, Warehouses→Demand, and Warehouses→Warehouses. The resulting distances are presented in three selectable tabular views, one per corridor, and clicking any row on the table renders the corresponding route on an interactive map. Pairs for which the OSRM engine returns no valid path are automatically replaced by the Haversine straight-line estimate and visually distinguished on the map. The computed matrix can also be exported as a spreadsheet. Fig. 6 shows this interface. Once the matrix is available, users proceed to model configuration. If Two-Stage Stochastic is selected in the model configuration (Fig. 7), additional fields are revealed to assign probabilities to the three scenarios and define the supply and demand perturbation errors — either from the WMAPE computed during forecasting or from manually specified percentages. The Pessimistic scenario reduces supply and increases demand to simulate resource scarcity; the Optimistic scenario applies the reverse perturbation; the Expected scenario uses the nominal predicted values without modification. Users may optionally activate the Pareto route filter to retain only the 20% shortest routes per origin node, reducing the number of decision variables at the cost of some feasibility margin in highly constrained networks.
